What this is, and why we built it

A working draft of a market-intelligence service for synthetic aviation fuel (e-SAF) in Europe: who is building, where it is stuck, and where the next plant could sit. Updated 26 Nov 2026.

What this is

Two prototypes built only from public data. The Europe e-SAF map shows announced projects and their status, Fischer-Tropsch units, ten candidate plant sites, CO₂ sources, and what the EU quota requires against what is likely to be delivered. The Adriatic fuel and hydrogen pages were the first prototype: regional fuel demand and industrial hydrogen buyers around the North Adriatic.

These are sketches, not finished products. Several inputs are assumptions or come from search summaries, and each page says which.

What the Europe map found so far

Announced is not built

55 projects in our list add up to about 2.9 Mt of e-kerosene a year, but only about 5 kt is built or under construction, at pilot scale. We found no large project with a final investment decision. The EU needs about 0.5 Mt in 2030, 0.9 Mt in 2032 and 2.3 Mt in 2035.

Delays decide the gap

On announced dates the 2030 and 2032 quota would be covered. With plausible delays and drop-outs the delivered amount is a fraction of that. The scenarios are our assumptions, so the page shows them openly rather than one number.

Sites are crowded and CO₂ is the bottleneck

Several good sites already have a developer or an owner with its own plans. For CO₂ we could document a large biogenic source for only two of ten sites (Vienna and Grangemouth, one of them modelled); the other eight have none documented yet, which may reflect poor public data as much as real scarcity.

Gaps in coverage

The source list has no project in Italy, the Balkans or Switzerland (Ukraine was checked separately: none found). In the Balkans large refineries already invest in hydrogen and bio-based SAF, but no e-SAF project turned up. This may be a data gap.

How it is built, and how far to trust it

  • Projects: Transport & Environment's list of e-SAF projects (May 2025), with e-kerosene volumes only (bio shares of hybrid projects are excluded). Statuses were refreshed in Nov 2026 from company and press pages, mostly via search summaries; low-confidence rows are marked.
  • Required volumes: ReFuelEU synthetic-fuel shares (1.2% in 2030-31, 2% from 2032, 5% in 2035) applied to Eurostat 2024 jet fuel use. The quota is EU-wide, so regional requirements are our pro-rata allocation, not law.
  • Scenarios: our assumptions about the share of projects at each stage that reach operation, delays, and ramp-up to full output. They are not forecasts.
  • Sites: a 20-point score (refinery, airport and pipeline, CO₂, power), reduced for competing projects within 150 km and for owners pursuing their own route. Only the first five sites were checked against sources for airport and pipeline links.
  • CO₂: company reports, an open database of biogenic CO₂ plants (CRONUS) and one university model. EU registry data (ETS, E-PRTR) has no biogenic column, so we could not use it to confirm volumes.
  • EU rules: CO₂ rules were checked against the 2023 Official Journal text; later amendments and Commission guidance were not.

Why start from projects and buyers

Investors follow offtake

A fuel plant is financed when someone credibly agrees to buy its output. Industry analysts name the lack of long-term offtake as a main reason projects stall. A clear picture of who is where, and who needs the fuel, is the strongest starting point for an investor conversation.

A list of investors is not enough

Press releases are visible to everyone and say little about timing. What matters is which projects are really moving, where capacity is missing, and who sits on a good site.

The price claim needs evidence

Any pitch built on a future price needs to show what has to be true for it. A live model of costs and inputs answers that and can double as the data room.

Relationships plus data

Strong personal networks already open doors. Data shows which doors are worth opening and why now.

What a service could look like

  • Project tracker: every European e-SAF and Fischer-Tropsch project with a status that is refreshed, and a flag when something moves, stalls or is cancelled.
  • Site screening: ranked candidate sites with refinery, airport, pipeline, CO₂ and power, and who already holds each site.
  • Supply and requirement view by region, with scenarios you can challenge.
  • Actor map: who works with whom (developers, technology providers, buyers, funders), to find investors with a reason to care.
  • One page a week and alerts, checked by a person before it goes out. It is a service run by us, so nobody on the receiving side has to operate anything.

Limits to keep in mind

  • Statuses and scenario assumptions are unvalidated; treat the numbers as an order of magnitude.
  • "None found" for CO₂ or projects means not found in public sources, not absent.
  • Rows 6 to 10 of the site ranking have not been checked for airport and pipeline links.
  • Plant locations on the Adriatic pages come from the account holder's postcode, so some rows need checking, and the EU ETS counts fossil CO₂ only.
  • Maritime fuels and small installations outside the ETS are not covered.

Proposed next steps

  1. Pull registry-level CO₂ data (EU ETS and E-PRTR, joined by facility) and check biogenic shares for the shortlisted sites.
  2. Verify airport and pipeline links for all ten sites and refresh the status of each project against primary sources.
  3. Fill the coverage gaps (Italy, Balkans, Switzerland). Ukraine was checked: no e-SAF project found.
  4. Test the delivery scenarios with industry experts, then decide whether a weekly brief is worth continuing.
